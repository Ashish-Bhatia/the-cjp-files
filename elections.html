INDEPENDENT COVERAGE · 19 OCTOBER 2026
ELECTORAL LAW · ECI · SUPREME COURT

What did the Supreme Court decide?

In Association for Democratic Reforms v Election Commission of India, W.P.(C) 640/2025, cited as 2026 INSC 564, the Supreme Court addressed the legality of the Special Intensive Revision carried out in Bihar in 2025.

Its official explanatory summary describes a 27 May 2026 ruling affirming the ECI's statutory power for that exercise, within the relevant election laws and procedural safeguards. The summary is not the judgment itself, and the ruling does not automatically decide the legality of subsequent revised forms or every disputed deletion in later states.

Why different voter counts matter

The Court summary describes the Bihar electorate before revision, the August 2025 draft exclusions and the September 2025 final roll. These figures identify different stages of the process. A draft exclusion does not necessarily equal a permanent exclusion of an eligible citizen.

Bihar 2025 measureReported numberMeaning
Before SIR7.89 crorePre-revision electorate in the Court's historical summary.
Initial draft exclusionsNearly 65 lakhDraft-stage removals; not independently classified here as ineligible or disenfranchised.
Final roll7.42 croreFinal electorate reported for September 2025. Additions, reinstatements and objections require separate reconciliation.

These Bihar figures must not be presented as national 2026 deletion statistics. The ECI's published rolls and notices are the starting point for constituency-level verification.

A later Form 6 controversy

Reporting on 12 October described the Supreme Court as clarifying that a modified voter-registration Form 6 introduced later was not approved or disapproved in the May Bihar judgment. That narrow point matters: constitutional authority for an intensive revision is not blanket approval of every implementation choice.

What CJP demanded

By October, CJP leaders were publicly calling for the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar over concerns about electoral-roll revision. Reuters' reporting also records the Commission and government defending revision as a means of improving roll accuracy and removing ineligible or duplicate entries. The correctness of any one removal is not established by either institution's public position alone.

How an audit should read official data

A constituency comparison must use consistent geographical boundaries, roll publication dates, additions, deletions, corrections and restored names, plus individual administrative and judicial decisions where relevant. It must avoid publishing people's private voter details. A headline national number without a consistent denominator cannot establish the fraction of eligible voters wrongly excluded.

The present sources establish the legal questions and reported political arguments. They do not provide a fully reconciled national count of wrongly excluded eligible voters, nor a definitive ruling on all subsequent 2026 processes.
