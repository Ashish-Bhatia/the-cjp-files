Violence on the streets, and the use of state power

An accurate record must examine alleged protest offences and the police response without treating accusations as verdicts.

Jaipur: violence directed at an organiser

At a 15 June Jaipur demonstration, Dipke was reportedly attacked by several people as he entered the gathering. Police were reported to have arrested five people. It is a significant incident of alleged violence against a protest leader. A conclusive reconstruction would require the FIR, footage with verified provenance, identified assailants' responses and subsequent court record. S04↗

Delhi: the 20 July confrontation

On 20 July, thousands tried to move from the Jantar Mantar area towards Parliament. Contemporary reporting described blocked routes, clashes, batons and tear gas. Police reported more than 170 people injured, including protesters and security personnel, and alleged extensive damage to vehicles. The injury count is attributed to police reporting; individual criminal liability is not established by aggregate numbers. S05↗ S06↗ S33↗

Police force needs the same test: what warning was issued, what alternative route existed, was force necessary and proportionate, who was injured and what can original video show? Some protesters engaged in confrontations, and some organisers urged peaceful action. Neither fact answers who committed every act. S34↗

The September Supreme Court settlement and its limits

On 1 September, the Supreme Court exercised Article 142 powers to quash the specified July student-protest FIRs and bar continuation of other FIRs for the same July 20–25 protest incidents, subject to an express exception. Delhi Police was permitted to register a fresh FIR concerning 2,873 named people described in its application, with their rights to legal challenge reserved. This permission is not a conviction, a determination of crowd membership or confirmation that facial recognition was correct. Saurav Das's CJP statement withdrawing the proposed September 5 march was recorded in the judicial order. S54↗

A subsequent Indian Express custody check found at least 25 suspect matches involving people who were in jail when allegedly identified on the protest site. The police said field checks remained pending. Claims about criminal participation or later prosecutions must therefore be tested against verified identity and evidence of individual acts. S55↗ S56↗

Another court-linked dispute concerned a Rs 5 lakh peace-bond notice served on law student Akshat Tripathi in Greater Noida after the Supreme Court order. UP Police later clarified that a sub-inspector, not the executive magistrate who issued the notice, had been suspended. Tripathi subsequently alleged the State's initial account to the Supreme Court was misleading. That allegation awaits the relevant signed orders, disciplinary record and adjudication. S57↗ S58↗

Jantar Mantar: what the Court ordered, and what police later did

The Supreme Court's 9 October Record of Proceedings in M A Baby v Union of India objected to blanket closure of metro and rail services as apparently overreaching and called for proportionate regulation. Paragraph 10 invited authorities to consider suitable alternative sites with amenities for peaceful demonstrators; it did not explicitly order the withdrawal of Jantar Mantar's designation. S64↗

After the police announced the venue's denotification and named Ramlila Maidan, Burari Ground and CBD Ground as alternatives, government counsel told the Delhi High Court on 13 October that this followed the Supreme Court order. On 15 October, however, the Solicitor General told the Supreme Court that a pre-existing Home Ministry committee report, not the Court's earlier observations, was the basis. The Indian Express separately traced that committee to 20 August. This difference between official explanations requires access to the committee recommendation, statutory standing order and contemporary decision files; it is not itself a finding of bad faith. S66↗ S67↗ S68↗

The Supreme Court's 15 October Record of Proceedings includes W.P.(C) 1357/2026, a challenge to the venue change, among connected matters. It issued notice, directed preservation of CCTV and other digital materials held by law enforcement, and ordered the Union to place applicable Section 163 BNSS orders on record. It also set case-management and hearing dates, including 3 November for the group containing this petition. Those are interim directions, not a final ruling about constitutionality or the merits of any individual arrest. S65↗

October: restrictions and preventive custody

Police detained CJP leaders before the planned October 10 Jantar Mantar event. On October 13, movement members reportedly entered a restricted site and were detained. Das and Ranka were later reported remanded for five days; remand is not conviction. The legality of each action must be judged from the relevant order, facts and governing law. S12↗ S15↗ S16↗

In Goa, the High Court ordered the release of seven protesters, saying in the account reported by Indian Express that detention may not have been authorised because a relevant BNSS prohibition order was absent. Separately, police registered an FIR concerning allegedly communal slogans at the protest. The judicial concern about detention and the police allegations concern different legal issues; neither cancels the other. S13↗ S14↗

Amnesty International called for Das and Ranka's release on 14 October, describing restrictions on peaceful assembly as a civil-liberties concern. Its position is an institutional advocacy assessment, not a judicial ruling. S49↗

Bar & Bench reported that Dipke was ordered released on 17 October after his separate October 16 preventive detention. On 18 October, CJP's Ratna Singh alleged the SEM proceeding concerning Das and Ranka was delayed and four sureties had been rejected. PTI separately reported a police non-cognisable report concerning an alleged threat to the magistrate by unidentified people. No original surety orders, message evidence or later release orders were verified here. Dipke's release must not be confused with the Das-Ranka case. S50↗ S51↗ S52↗

The custody timeline requires disaggregation. After their 10 October detention, Dipke, Das and Ranka were ordered released on 11 October. Das and Ranka were later detained following a separate 13 October incident. India Today published a text described as the 14 October Special Executive Magistrate's order, referring to the earlier peace bonds, notices under Section 130 BNSS and five days of custody pending further inquiry. This is not a criminal conviction; the signed original order, accompanying police report and recorded video evidence are still to be collected. S72↗ S69↗ S73↗

Reports concerning the 18 October release proceeding describe a lengthy wait, disputed surety submissions and an alleged threat that led the magistrate to withdraw from the hearing. CJP and Delhi Police dispute whether surety processing caused avoidable delay. The supposed message's sender is not established; reporting also varies over whether the police registered an NCR or FIR. The actual order sheets, original report and any subsequent release disposition remain unverified. S70↗ S71↗

The legal boundary

The right to peaceful assembly and expression is constitutionally protected, subject to lawful restrictions. A call for an officeholder's resignation is not itself evidence of unlawful overthrow. At the same time, peaceful political goals do not excuse proven threats, assaults, targeted harassment or property offences. The 2023 election commissioner appointment law is linked in the document archive to help readers distinguish questions of institutional process from questions of protest conduct. S27↗

Specific charge sheets, final judicial findings and full complaint and medical records remain to be attached before definitive determinations of culpability.
